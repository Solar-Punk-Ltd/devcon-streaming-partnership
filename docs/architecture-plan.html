    Superseded on components, not on reasoning

    An architecture review on 3 August 2026 changed several of the component decisions below. The reasoning in this document, the Swarm capacity analysis, the failure and threat models, the cost model and the go or no-go gates, is why the current design looks the way it does and remains the best account of it. The components are not current.

    What changed. The publish lease is gone, because two independent publish lanes make a forked feed impossible and leave nothing to arbitrate. The three shared origin gateways are gone, replaced by one prefetch node per feed so that nothing shared sits behind the stages. The coverage fleet is gone, replaced by four levels of 160 prefetch nodes reaching all 512 neighborhoods. The rented web2 mirror is gone, replaced by a standby stack we build ourselves. Pods carrying four to five stages each are gone, in favour of per-stage isolation. The viewer target moved from 20,000 to a 40,000 ceiling against an expected 4,000.

    The current component design is in the architecture explorer.

    Which sections are stale. Layer 1's pod argument, the node-fleet sizing, the degradation ladder and the YouTube mirror, and the two fleet rows inside the cost model. Each one says at its end what replaced it. Everything else, including every number, is current.

    Layer 0: the link we do not own

    Every live-event post-mortem comes back to the contribution hop, not the cloud.

    Contribution redundancy per stage

    New blocker question for EF

    Can your per-stage encoders emit two simultaneous SRT outputs to two different hosts? If not, we need an on-site relay box per stage, or we accept a single contribution path and label it an unmitigated single point of failure owned by EF.

    Cloud redundancy is worthless if the feed reaching the cloud has one path. Haivision, Videon and Teradek generally do dual-push. OBS does not do it cleanly.

    Local recording is cheap insurance and I would insist on it

    Recommended

    If the contribution path drops for 90 seconds the live stream has a gap and nothing changes that. But the on-site recording does not. Post-event we splice it into the Swarm archive so the permanent record is clean even where the live stream was not.

    Worth a recording box per stage on its own
    A contribution failure costs a live incident instead of a permanent hole in Devcon's archive.
    06

    Layer 1: one worker per stage

    The pod argument is kept because its reasoning is why the decision was hard. The review went the other way.

    The pushback Grounded in pac-bench

    Two servers per stage at 20 stages is 40 VMs, each with a media engine, uploader, Bee node, postage stamp and chequebook. That is 40 chequebooks to fund and monitor, and our own pac-bench work found chequebook funding is the single biggest lever on retrieval health.

    Forty of them is not redundancy. It is forty chances to have one underfunded at 11am on day two.

    Per-stage versus pods, at 20 stages
    DimensionPer-stage, 40 VMsPods of 5, 8 VMs
    Blast radius of one failure1 stage5 stages
    Chequebooks to fund and watch408
    Stamps to watch408
    Config surface408
    Failover pairs404
    Compute costsimilarsimilar

    The blast-radius argument for per-stage is real, but it is answered by the standby being hot. If failover works, blast radius is a 2 to 4 second discontinuity. If it does not work, per-stage isolation only means losing one stage instead of five, and you have already failed. So spend the effort making failover work rather than subdividing it.

    What we decided instead, and why this argument lost

    The review went the other way: one worker per stage, plus two standing spares, so 22 machines. The chequebook objection is what changed, not the blast-radius maths. The design now runs one funded publisher node per rung per lane, which is 160 chequebooks, four times the 40 rejected above as unmanageable.

    That is affordable only because the stamp manager automates funding and expiry as a lifecycle rather than a chore, so a chequebook stopped costing human attention. Once that held, the objection stopped being decisive, and 160 nodes buys something pods cannot: a full batch or a drained chequebook costs one rung of one lane instead of a whole pod. Note also that the second lane, not a hot twin, is what carries a stage through failure.

    Redundancy, concretely, and the split-brain trap

    The hard part is that two uploaders publishing the same feed would fight over the feed index, and split-brain on a signed feed is worse than an outage because it corrupts the archive. There are two ways to stop that: arbitrate who may sign, or make sure there is only ever one place a writer could run from. We do the second, because it needs no coordination at all.

    • Two independent lanes, not one lane with a standby. Every stage publishes twice, to two sets of feeds sharing no signing key, no feed and no postage batch. A fork between them is impossible by construction rather than policed.
    • One writer per feed, pinned to one host. The supervisor guarantees one process on that host, so there is no second place a writer could run from and no distributed lock to get wrong.
    • Standing spares rather than hot twins. A worker is stateless, so a spare has nothing to move across. Twenty workers plus two spares is 22 machines against 40 for one-to-one pairing, and it buys about 30 seconds of black instead of none.
    • On failover the spare picks up at the next index and arms a discontinuity marker. Our e2e scenario B proves the player handles discontinuity plus clean resume.
    • Because no standby transcodes in parallel, transcode compute is sized once rather than doubled.

    The top engineering priority if we say yes

    The media engine can produce an ABR ladder today. What does not exist is ABR over Swarm: the uploader writing variant playlists and the player switching through the Swarm loader. If it is not ready we ship a single rendition, which is a real quality compromise for exactly the mobile-first audience EF named.

    256 is the real number

    The network is European and shrinking

    Sourced

    Load has three dimensions, not one

    Conflating these is the most common mistake in this analysis.

    Write, read bandwidth, and connections

    The write side is a non-event. Twenty stages of full-ladder publishing is about 241 Mbps of pushsync and 7 new chunks per second per neighborhood at depth 9. The network will not notice us uploading Devcon.

    Direct-client retrieval load, no gateway — modelled
    Peak direct clientsEdge demandNet-wide, 4 hopsPer node avgvs ~10 Mbps Bee budget

    20,00044.0 Gbps176.0 Gbps56.0 Mbps5.6× over

    Even at EF's own conservative 4,000, direct retrieval exceeds what a typical Bee node is documented to want. And that is the average, which is the optimistic framing.

    The hot-chunk problem, which is the real shape

    One 2-second segment at 3 Mbps is about 750 KB, roughly 183 chunks. Each chunk hashes independently, so those 183 chunks land in 183 different neighborhoods. Twenty thousand viewers want all 183 inside the same 2-second window.

    Synchronized thundering herd on a fresh segment

    Swarm's saving grace is that forwarding nodes cache what they relay, so a popular chunk replicates along request paths and behaves somewhat like a CDN. That is why this is a risk rather than a certainty. But we have never measured the hit rate under a synchronized herd, and Devcon is not the place to find out with no rollback.

    Conclusion

    The gateway tier is load-bearing infrastructure, not a corner cut. With 10 gateways and 20 stages, Swarm sees about 600 Mbps of retrieval instead of 44 Gbps.

    The connection ceiling, sharper than bandwidth

    The in-browser node work surfaced a limit with nothing to do with bandwidth. weeb-3 holds up to 200 stable WebSocket connections.

    Connection exhaustion bites before bandwidth

    Modelled

    Browsers can only dial wss://

    What the fleet actually is now

    The tiering above was replaced by two roles and a much larger count: 160 publisher nodes, one per rung per stage per lane, and 640 prefetch nodes, one per feed repeated at four distances so every one of the 512 neighborhoods at depth 9 holds a node of ours. The shared gateway tier is gone entirely, because a CDN in front of the level-0 prefetch nodes does that job without any component sitting behind all the stages. Probes stay at four.

    Covering depth 9 rather than the depth 8 argued for above is deliberate: 512 sections cover the 256 anyway, and the thin neighborhoods at depth 9 are exactly where our own node is worth the most. This 800-node fleet is also why the cost model still carries a placeholder.

    Be honest about what full coverage is

    256 nodes, one per depth-8 neighborhood, packs into about 32 VMs at 8 nodes each. But it means we are substantially serving our own content from our own infrastructure, using Swarm as the addressing and verification layer rather than the storage market. That is legitimate and it is how most decentralized systems bootstrap, but we should not describe it to EF as the network carrying Devcon. Start at 32 and let measurement decide.

    What gateway "security" actually means

    Achievable and correct

    Do this

    Restrict what the gateway will serve. An allowlist of our feed owners and manifest references, refusing everything else. Stops the gateway being a free open proxy for arbitrary Swarm content, which is a real cost and abuse problem.

    Not achievable, do not imply it

    Careful

    Restricting who can watch. Swarm content is public by address. Anyone with the manifest can fetch from any gateway or their own node.

    Do not write "access control" in a doc EF reads
    They will think it means something it does not. For a public conference stream this is fine.

    Brave Shield caps WebSockets at 30, not 200

    Measured

    A 6.7× smaller pool very likely means much less parallel chunk retrieval, and Brave is over-represented among exactly the crypto-native audience most likely to opt in.

    Needed before we promise anything
    A measured weeb-3 number under Brave Shield, plus player logic that detects a constrained pool and demotes rather than stutters.

    This is a desktop proposition

    Assumed

    200 WebSockets, continuous chunk retrieval and erasure reconstruction on a phone is a battery, memory and thermal problem, and mobile browsers are hostile to long-lived WS.

    EF said mobile-first
    Hence Decision 4. Plan for a footnote, do not discover it in November.

    The largest gap between the POC and Devcon conditions

    Every current number comes from a well-replicated VOD, which was correct for isolating the client comparison and is the opposite of live conditions. A live segment is 2 seconds old, exists in one neighborhood, and has never been requested before. Expect the numbers to get worse and budget for that.

    How to ship the tier safely: caps, ramp and kill switch
    • Off by default. Enabled by the signed config feed with a percentage cap.
    • Ramp deliberately: 1%, then 5%, then 10%, watching probe latency, WSS inbound connections and the network health board between steps.
    • Desktop only for the first ramp. Mobile behind a separate later flag.
    • Automatic demotion to gateway on any SLO breach, per viewer.
    • Global kill switch reaching every viewer in under 30 seconds.
    • Every session reports TTFF, rebuffer ratio, chunk latency histogram, pool size, browser and shield state.

    That last bullet is the point. This tier is how we get the measurements the Devcon 8 talk is built on, and how Swarm learns what live streaming costs the network. Run it as science with a safety rail, not as a delivery commitment.

    A 20-stage gotcha that only appears at this scale Sourced

    YouTube is rolling out a cap of roughly 10 concurrent live streams per channel in 2026, enforcement uneven. Twenty stages needs at least two channels, each stage with its own scheduled broadcast and stream key.

    Plan for 2 to 3 channels with deterministic stage assignment, and verify the cap against the actual channels well before the event, because uneven enforcement means we cannot assume our channel matches the documentation in either direction. If EF publishes on Devcon's channel, the cap becomes their constraint.

    We build the mirror instead of renting it

    All of the above is moot. The standby path is now our own segments in ordinary object storage behind a second CDN, dormant until the signed config points at it. No YouTube, so no channel cap, no 20 extra RTMP sessions, and no platform deciding how our fallback behaves. It shares nothing with the Swarm path except the packager output, which is the property a fallback actually needs.

    The fallback ladder shortened with it: two tiers, not four. Livepeer is not in the design and YouTube is not the floor, because a rung nobody has built and tested is not a rung.

    Elasticity, and the one rule that prevents a budget incident

    Gateways scale automatically on concurrent connections and egress rate, not CPU, pre-warmed before each day's first keynote. Pods are manual and pre-provisioned, never autoscaled. Warm fleet is scheduled up days early, because reserve sync takes time.

    Hard ceilings everywhere. An autoscaler with no maximum plus a DDoS equals a budget incident. Every scale set gets a max instance count and every gateway an egress cap alarm.

    Threat model

    One threat is much weaker than it sounds, and it is the strongest thing we can say to EF.

    Censorship resistance holds up under a concrete attack

    Structural

    Chunk addresses derive from content, so consecutive segments scatter across the whole address space. Blocking one neighborhood blocks roughly 1 in 256 chunks, and MEDIUM erasure coding reconstructs from missing chunks anyway.

    To censor the stream
    You would need to capture a large fraction of the entire network, not a corner of it. No web2 CDN can offer this.

    Malicious clients hurt Swarm, not us

    Modelled

    20,000 fake browser nodes would degrade the public network, not our gateway path. Our viewers would be fine. But we would have handed out a template for stressing Swarm, and the reputational damage lands on Swarm.

    Another argument for the capped tier
    We should be the ones who discover Swarm's live-streaming ceiling, carefully, before someone else discovers it carelessly.

    Read every total here as a floor Assumed

    The fixed base carries $2,100 for 8 to 12 shared gateways plus a 32 node warm fleet, and that is not the design. The design is 640 prefetch nodes on about 80 machines, which on Azure at 200 hours is roughly $7,100 of compute before disks, and disks are not trivial for a node doing reserve sync.

    The decisive argument is load testing, not the event bill Modelled

    Three 6-hour runs held at 20k peak is 356.4 TB: $29,304 on Azure against $1,782 on a CDN. At the 40k ceiling it is $57,816 against $3,564. Load testing alone costs three to four times more on Azure than the whole event does under Variant D. Note these are held at peak rather than averaged at 45%, because a run averaging 45% is not testing the peak.

    Under Azure we would ration tests to protect the budget, and rationing tests is exactly how we reach Gate 2 in October without the evidence to say yes. Cheap egress buys confidence, and confidence is what the gate requires.

    Why a CDN works so unusually well here

    Content-addressed segments are immutably cacheable. A segment's address is its content hash, so it can be cached forever with no revalidation. That gives a CDN a near-perfect hit rate, which collapses the origin fleet to 2 to 4 VMs.

    CDN-fronted delivery with origin shield

    Origin shield is make-or-break Assumed

    With tiered caching the origin serves 2,601 GB roughly once, so origin egress is ~$312 and the total is $2,451. Without a shield every PoP pulls every object, origin egress jumps to ~52 TB, and the saving drops from 93% to 81%. At Standard Asia rates without a shield it falls to 51%. Verify the shield is on and measure origin pull volume during load testing.

    Is this just a web2 CDN, which we said we would not do?

    No, and the distinction is real rather than a rationalisation.

    Web2 CDN versus a CDN in front of Swarm
    PropertyWeb2 CDNCDN in front of Swarm
    Originprivate server, only the operator has the bytesSwarm, publicly retrievable by anyone
    Can a viewer bypass it?noyes, any gateway or their own node
    Can a viewer verify the bytes?noyes, the address is the content hash
    If the CDN removes it?it is gonestill on Swarm, still addressable
    Archiveseparate systemthe same chunks, permanently

    So the CDN is a cache, not an origin. Swarm remains origin, storage and archive, and any viewer who distrusts the cache can fetch identical, hash-verified bytes directly. That is defensible to EF and to the Swarm community, and it is exactly the on-ramp EF said they were fine with.

    Where each workload belongs

    • Ingest and transcode: Azure Central India. Needs proximity and reliability, egress is negligible.
    • Viewer egress: bunny.net Volume with origin shield, verified against India PoPs.
    • Origin gateways: 2 to 4 VMs anywhere. A near-perfect CDN hit rate makes this small.
    • Swarm fleet: Vultr, for both bulk coverage and India-local WSS entry. It pools its bandwidth allowance across the account rather than per instance, and it is the only low-cost option with Indian regions, so coverage and India-local nodes stop needing to be two providers. A full node earns BZZ far below $0.08/GB, so running it on Azure means paying retail cloud egress to earn cents.
    • Control plane: Azure. Cost negligible, managed services save real time.

    The Cloudflare trap

    Their free tier's unlimited bandwidth does not cover this workload. Terms restrict serving large volumes of non-HTML content on free and pro plans. A paid plan with the traffic profile agreed in advance is the only safe route.

    Postage and storage, the rounding error worth telling EF about

    20 stages at a single 3 Mbps rendition for 48 hours is 1,296 GB raw, about 1,393 GB stored after MEDIUM erasure parity. A full 4-rung ladder is 2,601 GB stored, and stamping it on two lanes is 5,201 GB paid for. The lanes carry identical bytes, so they resolve to identical chunk addresses and the network stores one copy while we buy postage twice.

    Single rendition, 1 year archive$2,175
    4-rung ladder, two lanes, 1 year archive$8,120

    The permanent decentralized archive of all of Devcon 8 costs between $2,175 and $8,120 a year. Still a remarkably good story, worth telling EF explicitly since it continues the archive.devcon.org precedent at a price no one will argue with.
